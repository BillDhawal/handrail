## KQ1. Why does "no model at runtime" plus an evidence chain matter to a bank examiner, an insurance regulator, or a compliance officer? What regulator guidance exists on AI agents acting in production systems?

### Takeaway
US bank regulators explicitly carved generative and agentic AI *out* of the April 2026 model-risk guidance and told banks to govern it with "broader risk management" while an RFI is prepared; NAIC's bulletin (24+ jurisdictions) and the EU AI Act (Articles 12 and 14) require logging, record retention, human override and a stop control. A system whose production path is a deterministic replay with hash-chained evidence and named human sign-off on irreversible steps maps directly onto what each of these regimes asks an examiner to see, and keeps the runtime out of the "generative/agentic model" bucket that regulators have said they do not yet know how to supervise.

### Cited Findings

**US banking (OCC / Fed / FDIC)**
- On April 17, 2026 the OCC, Fed and FDIC issued revised interagency model risk management guidance (Fed SR 26-2; OCC Bulletin 2026-13) that supersedes SR 11-7 / OCC 2011-12. It is "expected to be most relevant to banking organizations with over $30 billion in total assets" but may apply to smaller institutions with "highly prevalent or complex models." — [Sullivan & Cromwell memo, Apr 29 2026](https://www.sullcrom.com/insights/memo/2026/April/OCC-Fed-FDIC-Issue-Revised-Guidance-Model-Risk-Management); [KPMG reg alert](https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2026/model-risk-management-revised-interagency-guidance-reg-alert.html); [Davis Polk visual memo](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/visual-memo-key-changes-under-federal-banking-agencies-revised-model-risk)
- The revised guidance states that "generative AI and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving" and "are not within the scope of this guidance"; banks are expected to rely on "their broader risk management and governance practices" for those tools. The agencies said they will issue an RFI on model risk management for banks' AI use "including generative and agentic AI." — [Sullivan & Cromwell](https://www.sullcrom.com/insights/memo/2026/April/OCC-Fed-FDIC-Issue-Revised-Guidance-Model-Risk-Management); [JD Supra summary](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/federal-banking-agencies-issue-revised-5393200/)
- The revised guidance drops the old annual-validation cadence; for vendor models the "sound practice" is "developing an understanding of the model," ongoing monitoring and outcome analysis. — [Sullivan & Cromwell](https://www.sullcrom.com/insights/memo/2026/April/OCC-Fed-FDIC-Issue-Revised-Guidance-Model-Risk-Management)
- OCC's Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective (May 2026) supported banks' "measured approach" to generative and agentic AI, emphasised "guardrails and human oversight," warned of "lack of explainability, data privacy and data poisoning issues," and said banks "may consider expanding their use of [generative AI] and agentic AI for material financial decisions." — [Goodwin via JD Supra, May 19 2026](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/occ-report-signals-ai-governance-3047978/)
- OCC Bulletin 2026-29 (June 25, 2026) revised the Comptroller's Handbook "Lending and Loan Portfolio Risk Management" booklet and added three new sections: model risk management, third-party risk management, and automated credit approval processes. Examiners test model/third-party/automated-decision risk inside credit exams; the booklet does not name AI, so examiners fall back on general model and TPRM expectations and ask which named external standard (NIST AI RMF, Cyber Risk Institute FS AI RMF) governs generative and agentic tools. — [FTI Consulting, Oct 27 2026](https://www.fticonsulting.com/insights/articles/ai-risk-considerations-move-occ-credit-examinations)
- Interagency Third-Party Risk Management guidance (June 2023) covers planning, due diligence, contract negotiation (audit rights), ongoing monitoring and termination; the FDIC published a community-bank companion guide (May 2024). — [FDIC PR-47-2023](https://fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2023/pr23047.html); [FDIC community bank TPRM guide (PDF, dated May 1 2024; I could not extract text)](https://fdic.gov/resources/bankers/third-party-relationships/third-party-risk-management-guide.pdf)
- In September 2026 the Fed, FDIC, OCC **and NCUA** proposed a principles-based replacement for the 2023 TPRM guidance; comments due November 16, 2026. — [Skadden via CLS Blue Sky, Oct 5 2026](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2026/10/05/skadden-discusses-bank-agencies-proposed-changes-to-third-party-risk-management-guidance/); [Consumer Bankers Assn joint-trade roundtable](https://consumerbankers.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Joint-Trade-TPRM-Roundtable.pdf)
- A compliance vendor summarises FFIEC IT Examination Handbook procedures as looking for "audit trails of changes and enabled logging," periodic independent review of logging, and examiners sampling from "a complete population" of records with approvals, change logs and version control. **[vendor; I did not open the Handbook itself]** — [DeepInspect](https://www.deepinspect.ai/blog/ffiec-ai-audit-evidence)

**Credit unions (NCUA)**

**Insurance (NAIC)**

Insurers must "develop, implement, and maintain a written program for responsible use of AI systems" covering governance, risk management, internal audit, validation and retesting, "data and record retention," vendor diligence with contractual audit rights and cooperation with regulatory inquiries; regulators may demand document production on AI governance in market-conduct exams. — [Quarles, Apr 2 2025](https://www.quarles.com/newsroom/publications/nearly-half-of-states-have-now-adopted-naic-model-bulletin-on-insurers-use-of-ai); [NAIC adoption map (PDF, dated May 1 2026)](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/legal-adoption-map-ai-model-bulletin.pdf)

**Healthcare (HIPAA, CMS)**

**Federal government (RPA internal controls)**
- The federal RPA Community of Practice "RPA Playbook: Internal Controls" and GSA's internal-controls addendum exist as the de facto standard for bot identity, separation of duties and audit trails in agencies. **[URLs from search; fetch failed on both hosts]** — [Digital.gov RPA playbook internal controls](https://digital.gov/guides/rpa/rpa-playbook-internal-controls); [GSA RPA playbook IC addendum v1.0](https://origin-www.gsa.gov/system/files/rpa-playbook-ic-addendum-v10.pdf)

**EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)**
- Annex III point 5 lists as high-risk: (a) eligibility for essential public assistance benefits, (b) creditworthiness/credit scoring of natural persons (fraud detection excluded), (c) risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance, (d) emergency-call triage. Recital 58 gives the rationale (access to financial resources and essential services). — [Recital 58](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/recital/58/); [Mondaq "High-risk AI in financial services"](https://www.mondaq.com/ireland/new-technology/1800598/high-risk-ai-in-financial-services)
- Article 12 (Record-keeping): high-risk systems must "technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system," sufficient to identify risk situations, support post-market monitoring, and monitor operation under Article 26(5). The article page states Annex III obligations apply from "2 December 2027" (Article 113(c)) **[this is later than the originally legislated 2 August 2026; verify whether the "digital omnibus" delay has been enacted]**. — [Article 12](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/12/)
- Article 14 (Human oversight): the overseer must be able to "properly understand the relevant capacities and limitations," "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output" (automation bias), "decide ... not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output," and "intervene in the operation ... or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure." — [Article 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)

### Inferences
- The clearest regulatory argument for Handrail's design is *scope avoidance plus evidence*: because the April 2026 MRM guidance explicitly excludes generative/agentic AI and defers to "broader governance," a bank that puts an LLM in the production loop has no safe-harbour framework and must invent one; a bank that runs a compiled, deterministic capability can classify the runtime as a conventional automated process (the same bucket as RPA) and classify the authoring model as a development-time tool. The hash-chained evidence is what lets the examiner sample "from a complete population" of runs.
- GAO's finding that NCUA cannot examine technology service providers means credit unions carry third-party AI risk themselves; a vendor that hands the credit union a complete, tamper-evident run record for every execution reduces the credit union's own examination burden, which is a sellable benefit to the CU's compliance officer.
- NAIC's bulletin requirements (validation and retesting, record retention, audit rights, cooperation with inquiries) are satisfied by design if each capability is a reviewable artefact with a replayable evidence chain; the "proposes only" ladder maps onto the bulletin's demand for human accountability.
- EU AI Act Article 14's "stop button," "override or reverse," and automation-bias language are nearly a specification for Handrail's escalation ladder and human take-over of the live browser; Article 12 is a specification for the flight recorder. This matters for any EU deployment touching credit, public benefits, or life/health insurance, but only for the subset of tasks that are themselves high-risk "decisions," not every back-office task.
- The OCC's statement that banks "may consider expanding" agentic AI to "material financial decisions" signals examiners will soon ask *how* a bank bounds agent behaviour; "the model never acts, it only proposes, and commits require a named human" is a concrete answer.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the text of NCUA Letter 26-CU-01 itself; the AI-in-checklist claim rests on one unverifiable CUInsight article against three summaries that say AI is absent.
- No regulator has published guidance specifically on *agents operating legacy UIs*; everything above is inferred from model-risk, TPRM, and logging rules.
- The FFIEC IT Handbook text on audit trails was not opened (only a vendor summary). SOX ITGC expectations for bots were not researched (search budget exhausted).
- The EU AI Act application-date discrepancy (2 Aug 2026 vs 2 Dec 2027) needs checking against the Official Journal.

## KQ2. Banks and credit unions: legacy systems, recurring screen tasks, labor and RPA spend, regulator expectations, vendors, buyer

### Takeaway
Core systems (Jack Henry Symitar/SilverLake, Fiserv DNA, FIS, Corelation KeyStone) expose SOAP-era APIs that are thinly documented and cannot drive the cores' own workflow screens, which is why community institutions still run screen-driven back offices and buy RPA at roughly $8–10K per unattended robot per year; the cores themselves announced embedded agents in 2026, so the window for an independent deterministic layer is on the long tail of tasks the cores will not cover and on institutions that cannot wait for their core's roadmap. Hard labour-cost data for CU back offices is thin; the best public RPA case studies are 200 staff-hours/week saved from 5 bots and ~$600K/year from redeploying 8 people.

### Inferences
- The buyer is the COO / VP Operations (owns back-office FTEs) with the Chief Risk/Compliance Officer as the gate; at CUs under ~$1B the CEO often signs. The compliance officer's question will be "is this a model?" and the answer "no model runs in production; the model wrote the script once and a named person approved it" is the pitch.
- Core-vendor agents (Fiserv agentOS, FIS+Anthropic, KeyStone+Cotribute) will cover the top workflows on their own cores; the durable niche is (a) cross-system swivel-chair work (core + LOS + card processor + vendor portals + GSE/FHLB/CUNA Mutual sites), (b) institutions on older Symitar/SilverLake releases without the agent layer, and (c) anything the compliance officer refuses to let a vendor LLM touch live.
- Public RPA numbers suggest a mid-size CU can justify roughly $100–600K/year of value from a handful of bots; pricing a deterministic alternative at or below UiPath's ~$8–10K/robot-year equivalent, with evidence included, is the obvious anchor.

## KQ3. Insurance: agency management systems, carrier portals, "download" and rating tasks, vendors, regulators, buyer

### Takeaway
Independent agencies (which place ~87% of US commercial P&C premium per Big I, cited second-hand) run 15–40 carrier portal logins, and 172 of the top 250 commercial carriers have no quoting API, so quoting, dec-page/loss-run retrieval, endorsements and policy checking are still keyed by CSRs for 2–6 hours a day; the AMS incumbents (Applied Epic Conductor, Sept 2026; Vertafore Velocity agents, Sept 2026) just launched LLM agents *inside* the AMS, while browser-agent startups (Skyvern, Browserbase, Automat, QuoteSweep) attack the carrier-portal side. Applied explicitly markets that "each action is logged for E&O purposes," which confirms audit evidence is already a selling point in this niche.

### Cited Findings

**Systems and lock-in**
- Applied Epic is described as "the largest and most widely adopted AMS in the independent agency channel"; AMS360 (Vertafore) is the other enterprise incumbent; HawkSoft, EZLynx and QQCatalyst serve smaller agencies; all are quote-only pricing. **[vendor comparison blogs; no audited share data]** — [QuoteSweep AMS comparison](https://www.quotesweep.com/blog/ams-comparison-2026); [AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/insurance-agency-management-system)
- 172 of the top 250 US commercial carriers (writing $175B premium) have no quoting API; only ~11 carriers offer full small-commercial APIs (BOP/GL/WC); 131 of 500+ tracked carriers have online quoting portals; comparative raters reach only ~50–60 of ~2,700 US carriers. **[vendor research]** — [QuoteSweep carrier portal bottleneck](https://www.quotesweep.com/blog/carrier-portal-bottleneck); [QuoteSweep API vs portal](https://www.quotesweep.com/blog/api-vs-portal-insurance-automation)

## KQ6. Which vertical has the shortest sales cycle and a buyer who can sign without a 12-month security review?

### Takeaway
Independent insurance agencies and high-volume law firms are the only two segments found where the economic buyer (principal / managing partner) also controls IT, handles no PHI and faces no prudential examiner; hospitals are the slowest (BAA + SOC 2 Type II + HITRUST, with an 18-month academic-health-system anecdote), and banks/CUs sit in between because the 2023 TPRM guidance (and its 2026 replacement) requires formal due diligence before onboarding any technology vendor.

### Cited Findings
- Hospital AI procurement: BAA required; SOC 2 Type II and HITRUST "often preferred"; one multi-site study took ~18 months; security review "often becomes the longest part of procurement." **[vendor blogs]** — [Nirmitee](https://nirmitee.io/blog/epic-vendor-security-review/); [Censinet](https://censinet.com/perspectives/traditional-third-party-risk-management-fails-ai); [Paubox](https://www.paubox.com/blog/what-should-be-in-a-healthcare-ai-vendor-security-review)
- Banks and CUs: interagency TPRM guidance requires planning, due diligence, contract negotiation (audit rights, data), ongoing monitoring; FDIC published a community-bank guide; a replacement is proposed with comments due Nov 16 2026 and NCUA has now joined it. — [FDIC PR-47-2023](https://fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2023/pr23047.html); [Skadden via CLS Blue Sky](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2026/10/05/skadden-discusses-bank-agencies-proposed-changes-to-third-party-risk-management-guidance/)
- GAO: NCUA cannot examine technology service providers, so the credit union itself must evidence vendor risk. — [GAO-25-107197](https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-25-107197)
- Insurance agencies: AMS incumbents' agents operate "within Epic's existing security controls, so agencies do not need to connect a separate AI tool" (Applied's framing of the objection a third-party vendor must overcome); credential sharing with web agents is the named concern. — [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/applied-systems-builds-ai-directly-into-epic-to-automate-agency-workflows-591593.aspx); [QuoteSweep](https://www.quotesweep.com/blog/ai-web-agents-for-insurance)
- Law firms: legal RPA vendors claim 6–12 week fixed-cost delivery; MDK bought through its existing Ricoh outsourcing relationship. — [RaftLabs](https://www.raftlabs.com/services/legal-rpa); [Ricoh](https://www.ricoh-usa.com/en/insights/case-studies/manley-deas-kochalski-nintex-rpa)
- Vendor Skyvern claims setup of "2–3 hours for a first workflow and 1–2 weeks for complex processes" in legal. **[vendor]** — [Skyvern](https://www.skyvern.com/blog/automate-legal-court-filing-submissions-e-filing-portals/)

### Inferences
- Ranking by expected cycle: (1) independent insurance agencies 20–200 staff (owner signs; E&O evidence is a plus; competes with Applied/Vertafore agents that stay inside the AMS, leaving carrier portals); (2) high-volume law firms / legal process outsourcers on county e-filing; (3) physician groups, DME suppliers and RCM BPOs (BAA but no hospital CISO); (4) credit unions under ~$1B (CEO/COO signs but TPRM due diligence package needed: SOC 2, financials, BCP; Handrail's "no model at runtime, local evidence chain" shortens the AI-specific questionnaire); (5) hospitals and carriers.
- A credible path is to land in (1) or (2), use the evidence chain and compiled-capability review as the SOC 2-equivalent artefact, and carry those references into CUs where the compliance officer, not the CISO, is the gate.

### Gaps
- No quantitative sales-cycle benchmarks (days to close) were found for any of the four verticals; the ranking is inferred from procurement requirements, not measured.
- Credit-union vendor due-diligence timelines (months to onboard a new fintech) were not retrievable before the search budget ended.
